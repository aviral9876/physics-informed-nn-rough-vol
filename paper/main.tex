\begin{abstract}
Rough volatility models fit equity and cryptocurrency option markets well but are
non-Markovian, so they admit no finite-dimensional pricing partial differential
equation (PDE). The Markovian lift recovers a PDE by approximating the fractional
kernel with $n$ exponential factors, at the cost of raising the PDE dimension to
$n{+}1$, which makes the problem a natural testbed for mesh-free neural solvers.
We solve the lifted rough-Heston PDE with a physics-informed neural network
(PINN) that minimizes the strong-form residual, using a hard-constrained initial
condition, asymptotic boundary losses, self-adaptive loss weighting, a maturity
curriculum, and Monte-Carlo-informed factor sampling. We validate by decomposing
the error, in price space where the signed errors add exactly, into a
\emph{solve-error} (the network versus an independent lifted Monte Carlo at the
same $n$) and a \emph{lift-error} (lifted Monte Carlo versus the true model). Our
main methodological result is that the solve-error grows only sub-linearly in the
lifted dimension --- staying below about eleven price basis points up to $n{=}32$
over three seeds --- whereas a naive full-tensor finite-difference grid grows as
$50^{n+1}$ and is already infeasible by $n{=}8$; we are explicit that structured
grid schemes fare better but remain impractical for this PDE. The solve/lift
crossover yields a concrete rule for choosing $n$, which shifts to larger $n$ at
longer maturities. Calibrated to real Bitcoin options, the model returns a rough
Hurst exponent (about $0.09$) with strong negative correlation. Finally, a
parametric PINN that takes the model parameters as inputs enables gradient-based
recalibration; although the network alone is too inaccurate for production
calibration, using it to warm-start a short local optimization against the true
pricer reaches from-scratch fit quality at roughly nine times fewer true-model
evaluations. Mesh-free PINNs thus scale gracefully in the lifted dimension where
grids cannot, and are most valuable as a differentiable warm-start for
calibration.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} physics-informed neural networks; rough volatility;
option pricing; Markovian lift; curse of dimensionality; model calibration;
scientific machine learning.

\section{Introduction}

Pricing a derivative under a stochastic model amounts to solving a partial
differential equation (PDE). For most models this PDE is low-dimensional and
classical grid-based solvers are effective. An important and empirically
well-supported class of models, however, breaks this picture: under \emph{rough
volatility} the instantaneous variance is driven by a fractional noise with
Hurst exponent $H<1/2$, so the variance process is neither Markovian nor a
semimartingale and there is \emph{no} finite-dimensional pricing PDE
\citep{gatheral2018volatility,eleuch2019characteristic}. The standard remedy is
the \emph{Markovian lift}: the fractional kernel is approximated by a sum of $n$
exponentials, restoring a PDE at the cost of raising its dimension to $n+1$
\citep{abijaber2019multifactor}. Because $n$ directly sets the dimension of the
PDE while controlling a known approximation error, the lifted rough-volatility
problem is a clean, economically motivated testbed for mesh-free neural PDE
solvers and for studying the curse of dimensionality.

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) solve a PDE by minimizing its residual
at sampled collocation points, using automatic differentiation and requiring no
labelled solution data \citep{raissi2019physics}. Being mesh-free, they sidestep
the exponential growth in grid points that makes finite differences infeasible
in high dimensions --- the same motivation behind deep PDE solvers such as the
deep Galerkin method \citep{sirignano2018dgm} and deep BSDE solvers
\citep{han2018solving}. Two properties make the approach attractive for
derivatives: the physics (the PDE) is known exactly, so market data enter only
through the model coefficients; and, once trained across a parameter range, the
network is differentiable in those parameters, enabling gradient-based
calibration.

On the finance side, rough volatility reproduces the observed roughness of
realized variance and the term structure of at-the-money skew far better than
classical stochastic-volatility models such as \citet{heston1993closed}, and the
rough Heston model in particular admits a semi-analytic characteristic function
\citep{eleuch2019characteristic}. Calibration, however, remains computationally
demanding, which motivates fast neural surrogates. We use Bitcoin (BTC) options
as a testbed: the market is liquid, exhibits a pronounced negative skew, and
calibrates to a rough Hurst exponent.

Our contributions, stated conservatively, are the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A dimension-scaling result (primary, methodological).} The PINN's
\emph{solve-error} --- how well it solves the PDE it is given --- grows only
\emph{sub-linearly} in the lifted dimension, staying below about
$11$ price\bp{} up to $n=32$, while a naive full-tensor finite-difference grid
blows up exponentially and is already infeasible by $n=8$. We are explicit that
$50^{(n+1)}$ is the \emph{naive} grid; structured solvers do better but degrade
with $n$ and are not standard for this PDE, so the honest claim is that finite
differences are \emph{impractical}, not impossible.
\item \textbf{An error decomposition and a rule for choosing $n$.} We separate
the \emph{solve-error} (network versus lifted Monte Carlo at the same $n$) from
the \emph{lift-error} (lifted Monte Carlo versus the true model), report them in
price space where the signed errors add exactly, and use their crossover to
choose $n$; we show how the crossover shifts with maturity.
\item \textbf{Empirical calibration.} We calibrate rough Heston to real Deribit
BTC options and obtain a rough Hurst exponent with strong negative correlation.
\item \textbf{Fast parametric recalibration (applied).} A parametric PINN plus a
\emph{hybrid} (PINN warm-start followed by a few local Fourier polish steps)
reaches from-scratch calibration quality at roughly nine times fewer Fourier
evaluations; we report the parametric network's standalone accuracy gap honestly.
\end{enumerate}

We emphasize what we do \emph{not} claim. We are not the first to solve the
lifted (rough) Heston PDE with a mesh-free neural method: \citet{papapantoleon2025timestepping}
already do so. Our contribution is a specific combination --- a PINN residual
formulation, the solve/lift decomposition and $n$-crossover analysis, real
crypto calibration, and the parametric hybrid --- detailed next.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Mesh-free deep pricing on the lifted PDE (closest prior art).}
\citet{papapantoleon2025timestepping} price European options in (rough)
diffusion models by reformulating the pricing PDE as an energy-minimization
problem and approximating it in a time-stepping fashion with deep networks (a
``deep gradient flow''), demonstrated specifically on the lifted Heston model
and respecting the large-moneyness asymptotics and known price bounds. This is
the work closest to ours in both target (the lifted rough-vol PDE) and spirit
(mesh-free deep pricing). It differs in method: theirs is a time-stepping
energy/variational scheme, whereas ours is a PINN that directly minimizes the
strong-form PDE residual with a hard-constrained initial condition. It also
differs in scope: we add a quantitative $n$-scaling and solve/lift
decomposition, calibration to real market data, and a parametric recalibration
hybrid, none of which is the focus there.

\paragraph{Other neural solvers for rough volatility.}
\citet{jacquier2023random} solve the path-dependent PDEs of rough volatility by
interpreting them as backward stochastic differential equations and using a
reservoir-type \emph{random} neural network, which reduces training to a
least-squares regression with convergence guarantees. This shares our target
class but uses a fundamentally different mechanism (BSDE plus random features,
not residual minimization) and does not study dimension scaling or calibration
speed, which are central here.

\paragraph{Neural calibration surrogates (the calibration foil).}
The dominant paradigm for fast (rough-)volatility calibration is the supervised
surrogate of \citet{horvath2021deep}: a neural network is trained offline on a
large grid of precomputed model prices or implied volatilities and then used as
a fast pricing map inside a calibration loop. This is purely data-driven and
carries no PDE consistency --- the network never sees the governing equation.
Our parametric network is instead \emph{physics-informed}: it learns from the
PDE residual rather than from precomputed labels. This distinction is what makes
our hybrid meaningful --- the physics-trained network supplies a warm start that
a few true-model evaluations then refine (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}).

\paragraph{Foundations.} We build on PINNs \citep{raissi2019physics} and, more
broadly, mesh-free deep solvers for high-dimensional PDEs
\citep{sirignano2018dgm,han2018solving}; on rough volatility
\citep{gatheral2018volatility,eleuch2019characteristic} and its multifactor
Markovian lift \citep{abijaber2019multifactor}; and on classical transform
pricing \citep{heston1993closed,carr1999option} for our independent ground
truth. Two PINN training techniques we adopt are self-adaptive loss weighting by
homoscedastic uncertainty \citep{kendall2018multitask} and causal (curriculum)
training in the time variable \citep{wang2024respecting}.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Rough Heston and the Markovian lift}
Under rough Heston the variance is driven by the fractional kernel
$K(t)=t^{\alpha-1}/\Gamma(\alpha)$ with $\alpha=H+\tfrac12$ and $H<\tfrac12$,
which is non-Markovian \citep{gatheral2018volatility,eleuch2019characteristic}.
The Markovian lift \citep{abijaber2019multifactor} approximates
$K(t)\approx\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_i e^{-x_i t}$, introducing $n$ Ornstein--Uhlenbeck
factors $U^i$ with $V=V_0+\sum_i c_i U^i$. The discounted price $P(t,x,u)$, with
$x=\log S$, then solves the $(n{+}1)$-dimensional backward Kolmogorov PDE
\begin{align}
0 = {}& P_t + \Big(r-\tfrac12 V\Big)P_x + \tfrac12 V\,P_{xx}
   + \sum_i\big[-x_i u_i + \lambda(\theta-V)\big]P_{u_i} \nonumber\\
   & + \tfrac12\nu^2 V\sum_{i,j}P_{u_i u_j}
   + \rho\nu V\sum_i P_{x u_i} - rP,
\label{eq:pde}
\end{align}
with terminal condition $P(T,x,u)=\mathrm{payoff}(e^{x})$ and
$V=V_0+\sum_i c_i u_i$. All factors share one Brownian motion, hence the full
double sum in the $u$-diffusion and the cross term with $x$. The parameter $n$
is thus a tunable knob on the PDE dimension. We build $(c_i,x_i)$ by the
geometric-quadrature rule of \citet{abijaber2019multifactor}; the relative
$L^2$ kernel error decreases monotonically from $25.6\%$ at $n=5$ to $0.39\%$ at
$n=100$.

\subsection{Two independent ground truths}
We validate against two mutually independent pricers.
(i)~A semi-analytic \emph{Fourier} pricer solves the fractional Riccati equation
for the rough Heston characteristic function \citep{eleuch2019characteristic}
and inverts it by Fourier integration \citep{carr1999option}; it does not use
the lift, so it is the ground truth of the true model ($n\to\infty$). In the
flat-volatility limit it matches Black--Scholes to $3.7\times10^{-7}$.
(ii)~A \emph{lifted Monte Carlo} simulator integrates the $n$-factor lifted SDE
with an exponential-integrator Euler scheme that treats the stiff $-x_i U^i$
term exactly --- the mean reversions span roughly ten orders of magnitude for
small $H$, so explicit schemes diverge. It matches Black--Scholes within Monte
Carlo error. Because the two pricers approximate different objects (an ODE
versus the model dynamics), their agreement is a genuine cross-check. Crucially,
\emph{at finite $n$ the lifted MC is the ground truth of the PDE the network
solves}, whereas Fourier is the ground truth of the model; this underlies the
decomposition in Section~\ref{sec:decomp}.

\subsection{Physics-informed neural network}
We solve \eqref{eq:pde} with a PINN \citep{raissi2019physics}, a mesh-free
residual method in the spirit of \citet{sirignano2018dgm,han2018solving}. The
network is a $\tanh$ multilayer perceptron of $(\tau,x,u)$ with $\tau=T-t$, so
the terminal condition becomes an initial condition at $\tau=0$. We hard-wire
that condition through the ansatz
\begin{equation}
P(\tau,x,u)=P_{\mathrm{base}}(\tau,x)+\tau\,N_\theta(\tau,x,u),
\label{eq:ansatz}
\end{equation}
whose $\tau$ prefactor forces the learned correction $N_\theta$ to vanish at
$\tau=0$. The baseline $P_{\mathrm{base}}$ is a Black--Scholes price anchored not
at the spot variance $V_0$ but at the model's expected \emph{integrated}
variance,
$w(\tau)=\int_0^\tau \mathbb{E}[V_s]\,ds$, which captures mean reversion of $V$
from $V_0$ toward $\theta$; anchoring at $V_0$ leaves a systematic level bias
that the network would have to undo.

The loss combines the interior residual of \eqref{eq:pde} with asymptotic
boundary losses $P\to0$ as $S\to0$ and $P\to S-Ke^{-r\tau}$ as $S\to\infty$,
resampled each iteration. Two stabilizers matter. First, the residual and the
two boundary terms are combined with self-adaptive per-term weights via the
learnable homoscedastic-uncertainty scheme of \citet{kendall2018multitask},
removing hand-tuned weights. Second, a $\tau$-curriculum trains short maturities
first and expands the horizon, respecting temporal causality
\citep{wang2024respecting}. Finally, factor collocation is informed by a short
pre-flight Monte Carlo: rather than sampling $u$ in an arbitrary symmetric box,
we sample each factor from the mean and standard deviation it actually attains
under the lifted dynamics, which is essential for reproducing the skew.

\subsection{Validation protocol and error decomposition}
\label{sec:decomp}
The network is trained at fixed strike $K=1$ and prices as a function of spot;
the fixed-spot smile $C(1,K)$ is recovered by the call's degree-one homogeneity,
\begin{equation}
C(1,K)=K\,C\!\left(1/K,\,1\right),
\label{eq:homog}
\end{equation}
which holds because the variance dynamics are independent of the spot level.
Omitting this rescaling compares mismatched options and manufactures thousands
of basis points of spurious error (here $\sim\!2700$ vol\bp{} reduced to
$\sim\!286$ once corrected). We never report a lone ``PINN-versus-Fourier''
number; instead we decompose, per strike and in price space where the signed
errors add exactly,
\begin{equation}
\underbrace{(\mathrm{PINN}-\mathrm{Fourier})}_{\text{total}}
=\underbrace{(\mathrm{PINN}-\mathrm{MC})}_{\text{solve-error}}
+\underbrace{(\mathrm{MC}-\mathrm{Fourier})}_{\text{lift-error}} .
\label{eq:split}
\end{equation}
The solve-error is what training, architecture, and sampling can improve; the
lift-error is a model approximation independent of the network, shrinking only
with $n$. We report price RMSE in basis points of spot (where \eqref{eq:split}
is additive) and give implied-volatility equivalents where useful; the two
components have opposite signs and so do not add in RMS.

\subsection{Parametric PINN}
For instant recalibration we train one network that also takes the five model
parameters $(H,\nu,\rho,\lambda,\theta)$ as inputs, over a box around the
calibrated BTC values ($H\in[0.04,0.20]$, $\nu\in[0.10,0.60]$,
$\rho\in[-0.95,-0.20]$, $\lambda\in[0.5,4.0]$, $\theta\in[0.08,0.35]$); $V_0$ and
the maturity are fixed. Since the lift weights depend on $H$, they are
precomputed on an $H$-grid and gathered per collocation point, while the
parameters enter the network as exact continuous inputs. The number of factors
is fixed by the crossover rule of Section~\ref{sec:nconv} ($n=10$). The network
is differentiable in the model parameters, which enables gradient calibration.

\subsection{Calibration procedures and data}
We calibrate three ways: (a)~\emph{Fourier-in-the-loop}, differential evolution
with the Fourier pricer inside the objective; (b)~\emph{PINN gradient}, Adam on
the parameters using the parametric network as a differentiable pricer, with
zero Fourier calls; and (c)~\emph{hybrid}, the PINN's single-shot solution as an
initial guess refined by a few local Fourier polish steps (Nelder--Mead). The
objective is the vega-weighted implied-volatility RMSE against the market smile.
The market data are end-of-day BTC option quotes from Deribit, a cryptocurrency
derivatives exchange and data provider, retrieved through its public interface;
we set $r=0$ and infer the forward from the quotes, following the crypto
convention, and recompute implied volatilities from mid prices with a robust
Black--Scholes inverter.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Dimension scaling: sub-linear solve-error versus FD blow-up}
\label{sec:nconv}
Training the same architecture (width $64$, depth $4$) at $n=4,8,16,32$ on the
canonical BTC calibration at $T=0.15$ and measuring the solve-error against the
lifted MC over three seeds (Table~\ref{tab:nconv}, Figure~\ref{fig:nconv}), the
solve-error grows only mildly --- from $5.4\pm1.0$ to $10.7\pm2.0$ price\bp{},
about a factor of two over an eight-fold increase in dimension. It is not flat
(an earlier single-seed run misleadingly suggested so), but grows far more
slowly than the dimension. Wall-clock cost to the plateau stays bounded (about
$6$--$10$ minutes on a CPU; per-iteration cost roughly constant at
$55$--$62\,$ms) because the factor Hessian is vectorized.

By contrast, a naive full-tensor finite-difference grid on the same
$(n{+}1)$-dimensional PDE at $50$ nodes per dimension needs $50^{(n+1)}$ points:
$3.1\times10^{8}$ at $n=4$, $2.0\times10^{15}$ at $n=8$, and $1.2\times10^{56}$
at $n=32$. Even $n=8$ (about $16$ petabytes in double precision) is infeasible,
while the network trains every $n$ on a laptop CPU. We state the contrast
honestly: $50^{(n+1)}$ is the \emph{naive} grid. Structured schemes --- sparse
grids, ADI, low-rank tensor-train --- push the tractable dimension higher, but
their cost and accuracy still degrade with $n$ (sparse-grid node counts grow
super-linearly; tensor-train needs low-rank structure that the coupled
$\rho$--$\nu$ cross terms erode), and none is standard or validated for the
lifted rough-volatility PDE. The honest claim is that a competitive
finite-difference solve at $n=16$--$32$ is \emph{impractical}, not provably
impossible. Gentle solve-error growth with bounded cost, against this blow-up,
is the curse-of-dimensionality argument for the mesh-free approach.

\subsection{The solve/lift decomposition and a crossover rule for $n$}
The decomposition explains the record. A single ``PINN vs Fourier'' implied-vol
RMSE of $\sim\!114$--$127$ vol\bp{} at $n=4$ would read as network error; split
by \eqref{eq:split}, it is dominated by the lift. In the full configuration the
$n=4$ error is $37$ vol\bp{} of solve-error plus $133$ of lift (partially
cancelling to a total of $121$), and in the correlation-free case it is only $8$
of solve-error on top of $133$ of lift. The network already solves its PDE to
well under $50$ vol\bp{}; the apparent gap was model approximation.

Because the lift-error shrinks monotonically ($133,63,27,12$ vol\bp{} at
$n=4,8,16,32$; equivalently $16.4\to1.7$ price\bp{}) while the solve-error grows
mildly, the two cross. At $T\approx0.15$ the crossover sits near $n=8$--$10$
(Figure~\ref{fig:nconv}): below it the model approximation dominates and one
should add factors; above it the network's own error dominates and factors are
wasted. This gives a concrete rule for choosing $n$. The crossover also moves
with maturity: over a $T$-sweep the solve-error stays bounded everywhere
(roughly $1$--$9.5$ price\bp{} across $T\in\{0.05,0.15,0.5\}$ and
$H\in\{0.05,0.10,0.15\}$), but the lift-error grows with $T$ --- from $8.6$ to
$31.8$ price\bp{} at $n=4$ as $T$ goes $0.05\to0.5$ --- so at long maturities the
lift still exceeds the solve floor at $n=16$ and the crossover shifts to higher
$n$. Longer horizons need more factors.

\subsection{Calibration to real BTC options gives a rough Hurst exponent}
Calibrated to the Deribit BTC surface with a full-budget optimizer (differential
evolution, $60$ generations plus local polish; all $13$ maturities, up to $8$
points per maturity, $97$ points), the model yields $H=0.090$, $\rho=-0.792$,
$V_0=0.103$, $\theta=0.253$, $\lambda=2.567$, $\nu=0.300$, at a vega-weighted
implied-vol RMSE of $496$ vol\bp{} (Figure~\ref{fig:calib}). The Hurst exponent
is well inside the rough regime and the correlation strongly negative, both
economically sensible for crypto. We report the fit as deliberately coarse:
crypto smiles are wide and the lifted rough-Heston form is stiff, and a single
$496$-bp figure is honest about that.

\subsection{Parametric PINN and the calibration hybrid}
\label{sec:hybrid}
A single network covering the five-parameter box is necessarily less accurate at
any one point than a network trained there. At the box centre the parametric
solve-error is $11.0\pm4.7$ price\bp{} against $5.9\pm1.2$ for a single-point
network at the same $n=10$ --- about a two-fold coverage penalty. The penalty is
uneven: it stays near $9$--$11$ price\bp{} at typical points but rises to $47.7$
in the rough low-$H$ corner and $29.8$ at high vol-of-vol, where the PDE is
hardest. We report this honestly; a larger training budget would shrink the
corner penalty.

This limits standalone PINN calibration but not its use as a warm start.
Calibrating the BTC $T\approx0.15$ slice three ways
(Table~\ref{tab:calib}), the Fourier-in-the-loop optimizer reaches $23.7$
vol\bp{} using $2018$ Fourier evaluations in about $168$ s. PINN-only gradient
descent is fast (about $2$ s, zero Fourier calls) but poor --- $222\pm20$
vol\bp{} under the true model --- because it calibrates to its own imperfect
prices and, as an honest aside, because this single-maturity slice optimum lies
outside the parametric training box (it wants $\nu\approx0.87$,
$\theta\approx0.39$). The hybrid resolves this: seeding the local Fourier polish
with the PINN's guess reaches $29.4\pm7.1$ vol\bp{} --- comparable to
from-scratch on a $34$--$52\%$ smile --- in only $237\pm46$ Fourier evaluations
and about $23$ s, roughly nine times fewer Fourier calls and seven times faster.
The applied value of the parametric network is therefore as a calibration warm
start, not a standalone pricer.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Interpretation of the scaling result.}
The central finding is that the network's own error in solving the lifted PDE
degrades only gently as the PDE dimension grows, while grid-based solvers become
impossible. We deliberately state this in its weak, honest form. The solve-error
is not constant in $n$ --- a claim an early single-seed experiment appeared to
support but which three-seed statistics refuted --- it grows sub-linearly,
roughly doubling from $n=4$ to $n=32$. Likewise, the exponential $50^{n+1}$
count is the \emph{naive} full-tensor grid; sparse grids, ADI, and tensor-train
methods reach higher dimensions, so the defensible claim is that a competitive
finite-difference solve for the lifted rough-volatility PDE at $n=16$--$32$ is
impractical, not impossible. Even in this conservative form, the contrast ---
sub-linear error growth and bounded wall-clock against a grid that cannot be
stored --- is a concrete instance of the curse-of-dimensionality advantage that
motivates mesh-free neural PDE solvers.

\paragraph{The solve/lift decomposition as a general lesson.}
A recurring hazard when a surrogate replaces an exact solver is to validate the
surrogate against the wrong reference. The network solves the finite-$n$ lifted
PDE, whose exact solution is the lifted Monte Carlo, not the true ($n\to\infty$)
model; comparing directly to the true model conflates the network's solve-error
with the model-approximation (lift) error. We found the two are of opposite sign
and that the historical ``PINN-versus-truth'' gap was dominated by the lift, not
the network. The remedy --- validate against a same-fidelity reference and report
the two errors separately --- and the resulting crossover rule for the
approximation order are not specific to rough volatility; they apply to any
surrogate built on a controllable model reduction, and we would encourage their
use whenever a learned solver sits on top of an approximated operator.

\paragraph{Calibration and the role of the parametric network.}
The applied study is honest about a negative result and its resolution. A single
network spread over a five-parameter box is markedly less accurate than a
point-trained one, worst in the rough, high-vol-of-vol corners where the PDE is
stiffest, and calibrating with it alone is fast but unreliable --- it fits its
own imperfect prices, and the single-slice optimum can fall outside the training
box. The parametric network nonetheless earns its keep as a differentiable warm
start: seeding a short local optimization against the true pricer with its guess
recovers from-scratch calibration quality at about an order of magnitude fewer
true-model evaluations. The value proposition is therefore amortized ---
train once, then recalibrate cheaply --- with the true pricer retained in the
loop only for a brief, decisive polish.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Our evidence rests on a single market snapshot (one Deribit BTC surface), and the
parametric and calibration studies use a single maturity slice; timings are
single-machine CPU figures at moderate training budgets. The parametric network
fixes $V_0$ and the maturity, its calibration fit to the wide crypto smile is
coarse ($496$ vol bp at the surface level), and its solve-error carries a mild
directional bias (a slight over-pricing of at-the-money and call strikes that
grows with $n$). We study $n\le32$; the sub-linear trend need not persist
arbitrarily far.

\paragraph{Future work.}
Several extensions are natural: adding the maturity as a network input to price
whole surfaces rather than single slices; wider and deeper networks with GPU
training to shrink the parametric corner penalty and to test the scaling to
larger $n$; transfer to other rough models (for example rough Bergomi) and to
American or path-dependent payoffs; and a systematic study of the warm-start
polish across market regimes. We would also like to characterize the high-$n$
solve-bias and whether it can be removed by loss reweighting or a residual
correction, and to benchmark the mesh-free approach directly against structured
finite-difference schemes at moderate $n$ where the latter remain feasible.
